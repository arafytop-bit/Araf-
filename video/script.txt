## 1 · Cold open
Three weeks ago, the AI boom lost an election.
In state after state, voters picked governors who campaigned on freezing data center construction. Add it up, and about forty percent of America's planned data center pipeline now sits in states that want to slam on the brakes.
Ask those voters why, and you'll mostly hear one answer. It shows up once a month, in an envelope. Last month, Consumer Reports found that eighty-one percent of people in the same home for at least three years saw their electric bill go up.
So the fix sounds obvious. Fewer data centers, smaller bills.
Except the grid doesn't work like that. It's been gearing up for those data centers for years, and plenty of the power plants and wires meant for them are already locked in. Somebody has to pay for that, whether the data centers show up or not.
If the AI boom keeps booming, your bill goes up. If it goes bust, your bill can still go up. Heads, they win. Tails, you pay.
So how did your electric bill end up holding a bet on AI that you never placed?
## 2 · Stakes and setup
The answer starts with what you're actually paying for. Because an electric bill isn't really one bill. It's three bills stapled together.
The first is the electricity itself. The second is the wires that carry it to your house. And the third is something almost nobody has heard of, even though it's baked right into what you pay. It's called capacity.
Capacity is an insurance policy for the grid. It pays power plants to promise they'll be ready on the hottest afternoon of the year, when every air conditioner in your state kicks on at the same time. You're not paying for power you used. You're paying for power that's standing by, just in case.
Across thirteen states and Washington, D.C., that insurance gets bought at an auction run by a grid operator called PJM. It keeps the lights on for about sixty-seven million people, from Chicago to the Carolinas.
And the way that auction works matters. Every power plant that makes the cut gets paid the same price, the price of the last, most expensive plant needed to cover the forecast. So if the forecast jumps, the price doesn't just go up for new plants. It goes up for every plant that's already standing.
For years, that auction was about as exciting as a dentist's waiting room. For the year starting in June 2024, the price came in at twenty-eight dollars and ninety-two cents. That's per megawatt-day. Yes, that's a real unit, not a robot's vacation allowance. It just means a block of power held ready for one day.
Then data centers started showing up in the forecasts, right as older power plants were retiring.
The very next auction cleared at two hundred sixty-nine dollars and ninety-two cents. Over nine times higher, in a single round. In the part of Virginia packed with the most data centers, it cleared even higher, at four hundred forty-four dollars. The auction after that hit the highest price the rules allowed. Then the next one hit the ceiling again, at three hundred thirty-three dollars and forty-four cents. And PJM says that without the cap, that last auction would have landed somewhere near five hundred thirty.
Think about what that means. Prices didn't stop climbing because the pressure eased. They stopped because somebody bolted a ceiling on.
The morning after those first results came out, shares of two of the biggest power plant owners in the region, Vistra and Constellation, jumped by double digits. The people who own power plants had a very good morning. Your envelope just got a little heavier.
PJM even has an independent market monitor, a kind of hall monitor for the auction. It estimates that data center demand added twenty-nine point four billion dollars to capacity costs across four auctions.
Now, in fairness, capacity is still a small slice of a typical bill. PJM's earlier estimate was that these jumps alone could add one and a half to five percent for some households. But in that same Consumer Reports survey, twenty-eight percent of people said their bills had climbed by twenty to forty-nine percent.
That's because capacity is only the first way the data center boom reaches your mailbox. There are two more. And the third one keeps charging you even if the boom dies.
So here's the real question. When the grid builds for a customer who never shows up, who gets stuck with the bill? And is anything actually protecting you?
## 3 · The engine
Okay. The second way the boom reaches you is through the wires.
One big data center campus can draw as much power as a small city. Serving it can mean new transmission lines, new substations, new everything. Picture the world's most expensive extension cord, running from the power plant to the server farm.
And it isn't cheap. In California, the grid operator approved more than two billion dollars of transmission upgrades in a single area, the South Bay, built for data centers and other new demand stretching out to 2039.
Here's the twist. Under the rules most utilities follow, big transmission costs don't get billed only to the customer who caused them. They get spread across everybody. Utilities call that "socializing" the cost. Strip away the jargon, and it's a group gift you never agreed to chip in for, for a coworker you've never met. And the coworker is a warehouse full of servers.
It also helps to know how utilities make money. Under traditional regulation, a utility earns a return on the equipment it builds. More poles, more wires, more return. So asking a utility whether it would like to build more grid is a little like asking a golden retriever if it wants to go for a walk.
Now for the third way. The one that can keep charging you even if the AI bubble pops.
Finance people call it a stranded asset. That's a polite way of saying: we built it, and nobody came. Field of Dreams, but with substations.
And it isn't hypothetical. This year, the consulting firm Capgemini surveyed electricity executives and found that roughly one in five data center requests to hook up to the grid never turn into real demand.
One culprit is double-booking. A developer hunting for power might put in requests in several places at once, the way some people book three restaurants for one Friday night and only show up at one. California's consumer advocates have warned that speculative and duplicate requests like that can inflate the forecasts. And inflated forecasts get grid built that customers end up paying for, sometimes years before it's needed, or never.
Take a case from just outside Chicago. In February, federal regulators approved an agreement between the local utility, ComEd, and a new data center campus in DeKalb, Illinois. In March, the data center cancelled.
Or take Joliet, also outside Chicago. A proposed campus of almost two gigawatts. Roughly twenty billion dollars. When the utility asked for financial security up front, the developer reportedly offered a letter of credit for... one dollar.
One dollar. I've left a bigger deposit to hold a table at brunch.
The Union of Concerned Scientists went through utility planning presentations from this year. It counted about a dozen data center transmission projects that had been cancelled. Add in that disputed Joliet project, and that's more than five gigawatts of expected demand in Pennsylvania, Virginia and Illinois that's now in doubt or just... gone.
In Virginia, two developers walked away from a planned corridor of data centers in Prince William County that sat on Dominion Energy's transmission network. As of September, no replacement had been confirmed. And when a project disappears after the substations and lines are built, that equipment usually stays in what regulators call the rate base. That's the pile of equipment customers pay off over decades, whether anyone plugs into it or not.
Here's why all of this matters even if you've never been within a hundred miles of a server rack. PJM signs up power plants years in advance. The auction it held last December was for power to be delivered in 2027 and 2028. So when demand forecasts get cut late, the plants that were already lined up don't get un-lined-up. Those costs still get spread across everyone.
So that's three ways in: the capacity auction, the wires, and the stuff that gets stranded. Now picture the two ways this can go. Door number one: the boom keeps booming. Data centers keep plugging in, capacity stays expensive, and the wires keep getting built. You help pay for that. Door number two: the boom stalls. Projects get paused, cancelled or quietly shrunk. And the plants and wires already committed in their name still have to be paid for. You help pay for that, too.
That's the whole paradox. There's no door where you don't get a bill.
And door number two is about to get tested on a massive scale. New York became the first state to pause data center construction back in July. From January through July, local governments across the country adopted two hundred ninety-four data center moratoriums and bans. And more than fifty projects were cancelled this year amid local opposition, before the midterms even happened.
Maybe some of those projects deserved to die. That's a separate debate. But every pause raises the same question. What happens to everything the grid already started building for them?
This has happened before. And the last time, it got its own nickname.
## 5 · The history parallel
In the early 1970s, the Pacific Northwest was convinced it would soon need far more power than it had. The forecasts said demand would just keep climbing, year after year. So a public agency called the Washington Public Power Supply System decided to get ahead of it.
In 1971, it started borrowing money to build a nuclear plant. A few years later, construction began on four more. Not one plant. Five. At the same time.
The agency's initials were W-P-P-S-S. So people started pronouncing it "Whoops." I can promise you, nobody inside the building picked that nickname.
The whole plan was supposed to cost about four billion dollars. It didn't. Construction dragged on. Inflation took off. And when the agency started selling bonds for its last two plants in 1977, interest rates were under six percent. They did not stay there.
By the early eighties, the estimated cost of just those last two plants had soared to around twelve billion dollars. And the demand they were being built for? It never showed up.
Not everyone bought the forecast. Back in 1976, Seattle's city council looked at a twelve-volume study arguing the city could cover its growth by saving energy instead. The council voted six to three to stay out of those last two plants.
Everyone else kept pouring concrete. In January 1982, the agency finally killed plants four and five. One was about a quarter finished. The other, about a sixth.
Then it was time to pay. The local utilities behind those two plants had signed what are called take-or-pay contracts. Pay for the plant's power whether you ever get any or not. Those contracts ended up in court. And in 1983, Washington's Supreme Court ruled that many of them never had the legal authority to make those promises in the first place.
A month later, Whoops defaulted on two point two five billion dollars of bonds. With interest, the total owed was estimated at around seven billion. At the time, it was the largest municipal bond default in American history. The bank representing the bondholders went straight to court, and the legal fight dragged on for close to a decade before investors settled.
Two of the remaining plants were mothballed. Out of five reactors, only one was ever finished. And the debt on the plants that survived didn't disappear. It was backed by the region's federal power agency, which meant it got folded into what people across the Northwest paid for electricity.
Here's the lesson. And it isn't "nuclear bad." It's this. When you build giant, expensive things for demand that's forecast instead of signed, somebody eventually owns the gap between the forecast and reality.
Back then, the forecast was nuclear power for a booming Northwest. Today, the forecast is artificial intelligence.
## 6 · The escalation
Now, I know the obvious comeback. If tech companies overbuild, let them eat it. They're some of the richest companies in human history. They can afford it.
And honestly? They can. Whether they're able to pay was never in doubt. What's in doubt is whether anything makes them.
Most people already know which answer they want. In that same Consumer Reports survey, eighty-four percent said they'd support a separate rate category for huge users like data centers.
Some states are trying. In Ohio, regulators approved a rule in July 2025 that makes large new data centers pay for at least eighty-five percent of the capacity they sign up for, for up to twelve years, whether they use it or not. If they cancel, there's an exit fee. That's what protection looks like when it's written into a contract.
Notice what that is, though. It's a take-or-pay promise, the same kind of deal that fell apart in court in 1983. The difference is who's promising. This time, it's the data center on the hook, not your local utility.
And in March, the White House announced that major tech companies had signed a ratepayer protection pledge. They committed to pay for new power plants and the lines that serve their data centers, even if those data centers never come online.
Sounds great. But Ari Peskoe, of Harvard's Electricity Law Initiative, summed it up in six words. Quote: "Press conferences don't solve regulatory problems." A pledge isn't a contract. Until a utility files the terms and a regulator signs off, it's a promise with good lighting. And even where states do require long contracts, often ten to fifteen years, nobody has settled how big those payments should be.
On top of that, the biggest piece, transmission, mostly still gets split across all customers. The Union of Concerned Scientists says transmission costs tied to data centers add more than two hundred million dollars to bills across all of ComEd's customers.
So picture a tug of war. On one end, utilities that earn a return on whatever they build. On the other, tech giants that want power fast and as cheap as possible. And in the middle, holding the whole thing up? That's you. You're the rope.
And the rope is getting yanked harder. The Energy Information Administration expects America to sell a record amount of electricity this year, driven by data centers and manufacturing. It expects the average home price to rise from seventeen point three cents per kilowatt-hour to eighteen point two. One consulting firm, ICF, projects that residential rates across the PJM region could climb thirty to sixty percent by 2030. Put that on a hundred-and-fifty-dollar monthly bill, and the low end is forty-five dollars more a month. The high end is ninety.
To be clear, data centers aren't the only reason bills are rising. Aging equipment, storms and fuel prices all play a part. And a data center that actually shows up and pays its share can even help, by spreading the grid's fixed costs over a lot more electricity.
The danger isn't the data center that comes. It's the one that doesn't, after everybody else already paid to get ready for it.
So, back to the question from the start. Is anything actually protecting you? The honest answer is: sometimes. And remember Seattle's council back in 1976? Asking hard questions before the concrete gets poured is still the cheapest protection there is.
## 7 · The payoff
So what can you actually do with any of this? This isn't financial advice, but here are three things worth checking.
One. Pull up your bill and find the lines for supply, transmission and, if you're in PJM territory, capacity. Those auction results are already baked into what you pay. And if you rent, you're not off the hook. The cost still finds you, either on your own bill or folded into what your landlord charges.
Two. Search your state utility commission's website for "large load tariff" or "data center tariff." See whether your state demands minimum payments and exit fees, the way Ohio does, or whether it's still running on pledges.
Three. When your utility asks to raise rates, there's usually a window for public comments, and those comments go into the official record. Almost nobody sends one. Which is exactly why each one stands out.
