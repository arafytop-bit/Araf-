#Harald Hardrada.
Hardrada roughly translates to "hard ruler," which is the medieval version of putting "no small talk" in your bio. And if you're thinking, "Oh, a Viking. I've seen Vikings," no. You've seen TV Vikings. Harald was real, and his actual life is more ridiculous than anything a writer could get away with. If the Viking Age had a final boss, it was this guy.
So, in case this name is new to you, let me speedrun his résumé.
At about fifteen, he fights in his first real battle, next to his half-brother Olaf, who's trying to win back the Norwegian throne. They lose. Olaf dies. Harald is badly wounded, hides out at a farm deep in the forest, then flees all the way to what's now Russia and Ukraine. A few years later he turns up in Constantinople and joins the Varangian Guard, the Byzantine emperor's elite bodyguard, made up mostly of Scandinavian mercenaries. He fights all over the Mediterranean and piles up a fortune. And according to the sagas, he gets thrown in prison, breaks out, and helps blind an emperor on his way out the door. Then he escapes by sailing his warships right over the giant iron chain blocking the harbor. One ship snaps in half. His makes it.
Then he marries a princess of Kyiv, goes home, and becomes King of Norway.
And that's just the warm-up.
Because in 1066, the King of England dies with no son to take over, and three different men decide the crown is theirs. One is Harold Godwinson, the English earl who actually gets crowned. One is William of Normandy, who you might know as William the Conqueror. And the third is our guy.
So Harald sails for England with around 300 ships, smashes the first English army that tries to stop him, and takes York. At this point, he pretty much thinks he's won.
Now, here's the problem with this story. Almost everything we know about Harald's last day comes from one book, a collection of sagas called Heimskringla, written in Iceland by a man named Snorri Sturluson about 160 years after it happened. So, grain of salt. But what Snorri describes is insane.
On September 25th, Harald takes most of his army to a place called Stamford Bridge to collect hostages. And the weather is gorgeous. It's so warm that, according to the saga, his men leave their armor back at the ships. They walk out with just shields, helmets, spears and swords, and the saga literally says they were all very merry.
Meanwhile, the English king has been racing north, pushing his army something like 40 miles a day. Nobody warns the Norwegians. So while Harald's men are chilling in the sun in what are basically t-shirts, a cloud of dust rises on the horizon, and underneath it, the shine of a whole lot of armor.
So what does the scariest Viking alive do when an entire English army shows up and he left his armor in the car? For that, we have to go to the saga itself.
First, Harald rides out to inspect his troops, and his horse stumbles and throws him to the ground, in front of everybody. He jumps right back up and says, quote, "A fall is lucky for a traveller." Which is the most powerful possible way to cover for eating dirt.
The English king sees all this from across the field and asks who the big man in the blue tunic is, the one who just fell off his horse. They tell him it's the King of Norway. And he says, roughly: a great man, and a stately one, but I think his luck has left him.
Then twenty English riders come forward to talk. One of them offers Harald's ally a huge chunk of England to switch sides. And when he's asked what Harald gets, the rider says: seven feet of English ground, or a bit more, since he's taller than other men. Translation: the only English land you're getting is your grave.
Oh, and that rider? According to the saga, it was the English king himself.
Harald chooses violence anyway. The saga says he charged out ahead of his own lines, swinging with both hands. And then an arrow hits him in the windpipe. That's it. The last great Viking king dies on an English field, without his armor, on the one day he decided armor was optional.
And the English have their own version of this battle. A scribe added it to the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle decades later, so it's not exactly bodycam footage. But it says a single Norwegian held the bridge by himself, so the whole English army couldn't cross. A later historian claims he killed about forty men. He only went down when an Englishman got underneath the bridge and stabbed up through the gap. Later retellings even put that guy in a floating barrel.
It gets worse for Norway. Reinforcements sprint over from the ships in full armor, in the heat, and arrive so exhausted they can barely fight. The Norwegians had shown up with around 300 ships. They needed 24 to carry the survivors home.
But here's the craziest part. Three days later, William of Normandy lands in the south. Harold Godwinson has to drag his exhausted army all the way back down, and nineteen days after Stamford Bridge, he dies at the Battle of Hastings. So Harald's death is often used to mark the end of the Viking Age, and it helped set up the Norman Conquest, which is a big reason English is full of French words today.
Not bad for a guy who lost to the weather.
#Attila the Hun.
Here's the thing about Attila: almost everything we know about him was written by people who hated him. Our best source is a Roman diplomat named Priscus, who visited Attila's court in 449 and had dinner with him. Which is a sentence I need you to appreciate.
If you're fuzzy on the Huns, they were horse archers from the steppe who rode into Europe and scared the Roman Empire so badly that emperors started paying them to go away. Under Attila, the bill hit 2,100 pounds of gold. Per year. He wasn't conquering Rome so much as charging it a subscription.
He started out sharing the throne with his brother, Bleda, until Bleda died, and one ancient writer says that was thanks to Attila's treachery. After that, he invaded what's now France, invaded Italy, and became the most feared man in Europe.
He even had a legendary weapon. Supposedly, a herdsman noticed one of his cows limping, followed the trail of blood, and found a sword buried in the grass. Attila decided it was the sword of the war god himself, and proof he was destined to rule the world.
And yet, according to Priscus, at that dinner the guests ate off silver plates and drank from gold and silver cups, while Attila ate plain meat off a wooden plate and drank from a wooden cup. Final boss energy, starter-gear equipment.
So why is a list of epic deaths including a guy who died of a nosebleed? Because, yes, that's how Attila died.
In 453, he married a young woman named Ildico. He'd had a lot of wives, so for him this was a regular Tuesday. He partied hard at the wedding feast, went to bed drunk, and lay on his back. During the night, his nose started bleeding, the blood ran down his throat, and he choked on it.
The next morning, his attendants got suspicious, broke down the door, and found the most feared man in Europe dead without a single wound, and his new wife crying beneath her veil. Some later writers claimed she killed him, but our earliest account blames the nosebleed.
So that's the death. Embarrassing. But the funeral? The funeral is why he's on this list.
According to the historian Jordanes, working from Priscus, Attila's warriors cut off their hair and slashed their own faces, so that the greatest warrior of all would be mourned not with tears, but with the blood of men.
Then they sealed his body inside three coffins: one of gold, one of silver, and one of iron. Gold and silver for the riches he took. Iron for the nations he crushed. They buried him at night with weapons and treasure, and then, so that no one would ever find it, they killed the men who dug the grave.
And it worked. More than fifteen hundred years later, nobody has found Attila's tomb.
Within about a year, the peoples he'd conquered rose up against his sons, and his empire fell apart. So Attila had the least epic death in history, and the most epic funeral. He walked so every heavy metal album cover could run.
#Marcus Licinius Crassus.
No, not "crass," like your uncle at Thanksgiving. Although, fun fact: in Latin, crassus means "fat" or "thick." Which is a wild last name for the richest man in Rome. And that's exactly who this was.
How rich? According to the Greek biographer Plutarch, Crassus noticed that Roman buildings were constantly catching fire or collapsing. So he bought more than 500 enslaved builders and architects. Then, whenever a house went up in flames, he'd show up and offer to buy it, and the houses next to it, for almost nothing, while the owners were panicking. That's how he ended up owning a huge chunk of the city. Rome's richest man got rich buying your house while it was on fire.
If you've heard of Spartacus, the gladiator who led a massive slave revolt, Crassus is the general who crushed it. Afterward, around six thousand captured rebels were crucified along the road to Rome. That's who we're dealing with.
But Crassus had a problem. He had money. What he didn't have was glory. His two political partners were Pompey and Julius Caesar, two of the most famous generals who ever lived, and he could not stand being the one known for his bank account. So at around sixty years old, instead of buying a sports chariot, Crassus decided his midlife crisis would be invading the Parthian Empire, a superpower that ruled what's now Iran and Iraq.
It did not go well. At all.
In 53 BC, near a town called Carrhae, his army ran into Parthian horse archers on open ground. Picture this: you're a Roman legionary in a tight formation behind your shield, waiting for the enemy to run out of arrows. That's the plan. That's the entire plan. Then, according to Plutarch, the Romans spotted camels loaded with fresh arrows, so the archers could just ride back and reload. The arrows were never going to run out.
The Parthians would even fake a retreat, twist around in the saddle, and fire backward. That move became known as the Parthian shot, and a lot of people think it's where we get the phrase "parting shot."
Crassus's son led a charge to drive the archers off. The Parthians came back carrying his son's head on a spear. Eventually Crassus was lured into peace talks, a scuffle broke out, and he was killed. Plutarch says about twenty thousand Romans died in the campaign, and another ten thousand were captured. Rome even lost its sacred eagle standards, and it took more than thirty years of diplomacy to get them back.
And that's not even the wild part.
The Parthians cut off his head and right hand and sent them to their king, who was at a royal wedding feast, watching a performance of a Greek tragedy called the Bacchae. The next scene features a mother carrying the severed head of her own son. So the lead actor grabs Crassus's actual head and uses it as the prop. Then the Parthian who killed Crassus jumps up, snatches the head, and insists he should be the one saying the line, since he's the one who did it. The king loved it so much he gave the actor a fortune.
The richest man in Rome ended up as a stage prop.
Meanwhile, the Parthian general who beat him threw a fake Roman victory parade, starring a captive who looked like Crassus, dressed in a woman's royal robe and told to answer to Crassus's name.
Oh, and another historian, Cassius Dio, writing more than 250 years later, says the Parthians poured molten gold into his mouth to mock his greed. Even Dio only says it's what "some say," so it might be legend. Either way, the moral writes itself. Crassus spent his whole life chasing gold, and according to the story, he finally got more than he could swallow.
#Arrichion of Phigalia.
If Harald going into battle without armor felt hardcore, this guy makes him look cautious. Because unlike everyone else on this list, Arrichion wasn't a king or a general. He was an athlete. And not in a normal sport. He competed in pankration, at the ancient Olympics. It had been part of the Games since 648 BC, and the Greeks were obsessed with it. Pankration champions were the superstars of their day.
If pankration is new to you, think MMA, but the rulebook was written by someone who had never heard of lawsuits. Here's what was illegal: biting, and gouging out eyes. That's it. That's the whole list. Here's what was legal: punching, kicking, choking, joint locks, kicking a guy in the stomach, and bending back your opponent's fingers until they snapped. One famous champion won by doing exactly that. And in Sparta, they reportedly didn't even bother banning the biting and gouging.
No weight classes. No rounds. No time limit. You won when the other guy gave up. And the prize? Not gold. Not money. A wreath of olive leaves. In my completely unqualified opinion, this was the most dangerous sport ever to hand out a trophy.
And Arrichion wasn't just good at it. He was the guy. He won the Olympic pankration in 572 BC. Then again in 568. And in 564 BC, he came back for a third straight title. He fights his way through the field and makes it to the final.
And in the final, it all goes wrong. His opponent gets a grip first, wraps his legs around Arrichion's waist, and squeezes his neck. Arrichion is fading. He's about to black out.
And this is where his trainer, a man named Eryxias, yells something that, according to the writer Philostratus, kept him from giving up. Quote: "What a noble epitaph: He was never defeated at Olympia."
An epitaph is the line they carve on your gravestone. So his coach is basically yelling, "Hey, if you die right now, your tombstone is going to look amazing."
Respectfully: what? Your fighter is being strangled to death, and your pep talk is about his funeral? That's not coaching. That's a threat.
But it worked. With the last strength he had, Arrichion wrenched his opponent's foot out of joint. The sources disagree on whether it was a toe or an ankle, but the pain was so bad that the opponent gave up. And at that same moment, Arrichion died from the chokehold.
Which left the judges with an awkward situation. The winner was dead. The loser was alive, and holding his foot. History didn't even save the loser's name. Our main source, a Greek travel writer named Pausanias, literally calls him "whoever he was."
So the judges did the only fair thing. They crowned the corpse. Arrichion's body received the olive wreath, and he was declared Olympic champion for the third time. Pausanias says the win came partly from the fairness of the judges, and partly from Arrichion's own courage.
About seven hundred years after the fight, Pausanias visited Arrichion's hometown and saw his statue still standing in the marketplace. A statue many believe is that very one now sits in the museum at Olympia.
As far as we know, Arrichion is the only athlete in history to win an Olympic final after dying. Which sounds impossible to top.
But our last guy? He pulled it off with an entire fleet.

#Yi Sun-sin.
Quick question. Who's the greatest admiral who ever lived? If you said Horatio Nelson, the British are very proud of you. If you said Captain Hook, please leave. But there's a name a lot of naval history nerds would put on that list, and most people outside Korea have never heard it. And his death is, in my opinion, the most epic death in recorded history.
In 1592, Japan invades Korea with well over 150,000 soldiers. On land, it's a disaster. The capital, Seoul, falls in about three weeks. But at sea, one Korean admiral starts winning. Then he keeps winning. He brings out ships the Japanese had never seen before, called turtle ships: covered decks studded with iron spikes so nobody could board, cannons on every side, and a dragon's head on the front.
At Hansan Island, he lured a Japanese fleet into open water, wrapped his ships around it in a giant U-shape called the crane wing formation, and wiped most of it out. His wins cut Japan's supply lines by sea, which is a big reason the invasion stalled. By most counts, Yi fought around twenty-three naval battles. He's usually described as never losing a single one.
And as a reward, his own government arrested him. Again. Earlier in his career, he'd already been falsely accused, stripped of his rank, and sent to serve as a common soldier.
In 1597, thanks to court politics and an enemy tip he suspected was a trap, Yi didn't follow an order to attack. So he was hauled to the capital, tortured, nearly executed, and demoted to a common foot soldier for the second time. While he was on his way to report for duty, his mother died, and he had to keep going instead of staying to mourn her.
Meanwhile, his replacement sailed almost the entire Korean navy straight into a Japanese ambush. Out of a fleet that had grown to more than 160 warships under Yi, about a dozen escaped.
So the king, out of options, gave Yi his old job back. And things looked so hopeless that the king suggested giving up on the navy entirely. Yi wrote back with one of the most legendary lines in Korean history: "Your servant still has twelve ships."
Twelve. Plus one more they patched up later. Thirteen ships, against an entire navy.
At the Battle of Myeongnyang, he faced at least 133 Japanese warships, by his own count, with maybe 200 more support ships behind them. Before the battle, he told his officers: "Those who seek death shall live, and those who seek life shall die."
He picked his battlefield: a narrow strait with some of the fiercest tidal currents in Korea. And here's the part that gets me. When the fighting started, most of his captains hung back, terrified. So for a while, Yi's flagship held off the Japanese front line pretty much alone, until the rest of his ships found the nerve to join in. Then the tide turned. The Japanese ships started drifting backward and crashing into each other, and Yi's thirteen ships tore into them. When it was over, 31 Japanese ships had been wrecked. Yi didn't lose a single ship.
Weeks later, Japanese troops raided his family's hometown and killed his youngest son. In his war diary, Yi wrote that it should have been the other way around: he should have died, and his son should have lived. And then he kept fighting anyway.
Which brings us to 1598. Japan's ruler has died, and the invaders are trying to sail home. Plenty of commanders would have let them go. Yi didn't. On December 16th, around two in the morning, he and his Chinese allies hit the Japanese fleet in the Noryang Strait.
The fighting raged until daylight. And as the Japanese ships started to flee, a bullet struck Yi in the chest. He knew right away. And his last words were: "The battle is at its height. Do not announce my death." He was fifty-three years old.
His son and his nephew carried his body into his cabin. Then his nephew picked up the war drum and kept beating it, so the whole fleet would believe the admiral was still giving orders. Nobody knew. Not his sailors. Not the enemy. The fleet fought on and won. Korean records claim hundreds of Japanese ships were destroyed. It was the last major battle of a seven-year war.
Afterward, the Chinese admiral Chen Lin came over to thank Yi for coming to his rescue. When they told him Yi was dead, it's said he fell to the deck three times, beating his chest and weeping.
Think about that. Arrichion won one fight after he died. Yi Sun-sin won the final battle of a seven-year war, and his own fleet didn't even know he was gone. Korea gave him the title Chungmugong, roughly "Lord of Loyalty and Warfare." One of the most-watched movies in South Korean history is about his thirteen ships. His face is on the 100-won coin, and his statue stands in the heart of Seoul. He earned all of it.
